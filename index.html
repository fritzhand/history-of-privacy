A History of Privacy  ·  antiquity – 2026

Who Writes
the Terms?

For most of history, privacy was a wall, a seal or a law someone else enforced. Online, it became a policy the other side wrote and you clicked to accept. A new IEEE standard, 7012 — MyTerms — turns that around: you propose the terms; if the site or service agrees, both keep the record. This is the long road to that reversal, and why it matters now that AI agents act for us.

    Data Protection  ·  1970 – 1993

    The Law Catches Up with the Computer

    The first data-protection law was passed in the German state of Hesse in 1970. By 1981, principles written in Washington, Paris and Strasbourg had given the world a shared vocabulary for records about people. They are addressed to the organisation that holds the data — though the first of them already said the person should help set the terms.

    Cookies & Consent  ·  1994 – 2012

    The Consent Machine

    The web settled on "notice and choice": the organisation publishes a policy and the person is deemed to have agreed. The research on how that works in practice is consistent — the policies are long and getting longer, most people do not read them, and most say they feel they have little control.

    The Destination  ·  IEEE 7012-2025

    MyTerms: The Person as First Party

    IEEE 7012 is a standard for machine-readable personal privacy terms. Instead of a site presenting its policy and asking you to accept, you — or software acting for you — proffer one of a small set of standard agreements kept in a public roster; the site's software accepts it, declines or offers one alternative from the same roster, and once they agree, each side keeps an identical record.

    The Principles Built into the Agreements

    The Launch Agreements

    Two relationship agreements for ongoing service, and three for one-off contributions of data. MyTerms says each comes in plain language, in machine-readable form and as a legal text — the three readings the standard requires.

    The Agentic Turn  ·  2023 – today

    Why It Matters Now: Terms for the Age of Agents

    AI agents now browse, buy, book and fill in forms for people. Each of those errands meets someone else's terms. An agent cannot meaningfully read a cookie banner, and a person cannot review every agreement an agent makes on their behalf. What an agent needs is its principal's terms in a form it can present, and a record that the other side agreed to them.

      About

      What This Is, and How to Read It

      An open-data history of privacy, told as a history of who gets to set the terms.

      Privacy is old; the idea that a person could state their own privacy terms to an organisation, in a form a machine can read and, its backers say, a court can enforce, is new. This project follows the line between the two — with a world map, a set of charts, a year scrubber, a lineage of machine-readable privacy and a rights-cleared picture archive — and it shows its working for every claim on the page.

      It is a domain fork of Indigo: The Blue That Dyed the World and Downtown Tampa: same architecture, same citation standard, a different subject. The site is static: no build step for the reader, no backend, no cookies and no analytics. Everything it knows lives in one cited data file, compiled from the research notes in the repository.

      How the evidence is graded

      Every claim carries a source object naming the institution, the document, the date and a status, and most carry the exact sentence from the source that supports them. The research pass fetched each cited page it could reach and checked the quoted sentence against it (the few it could not are marked Pending); a second, independent pass tried to refute every record.

      • Confirmed The cited page states exactly this, and the quote was checked against the page. Hero and footer numbers must be confirmed.
      • Pending The source is the right one or a credible secondary account, but the claim has not yet been read in the primary record. Treat as provisional.
      • Derived Computed or editorial: an era boundary, a cumulative count, an illustrative scenario. The note names the inputs. Do not cite these as primary figures.

      The Source Audit prints the live tally. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is printed, not averaged.

      A note on the destination

      This study argues that IEEE 7012 matters. It tries to earn that argument rather than assume it: the case for the standard in the age of agents is set out claim by claim with evidence, the scenario is labelled as an illustration, and the limits — adoption, enforcement, and the fate of the machine-readable efforts that came before — have their own section.

      The study area

      Privacy as a claim about information and the self — confidentiality, the home, correspondence, images, records and data — from the earliest written rules to September 2026. Bodily and reproductive privacy appear where they shaped the law of information. The emphasis falls on Europe and North America, where most of the law this story needs was written; other traditions appear where they could be properly sourced.
